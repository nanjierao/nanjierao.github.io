Interest-based learning connects instruction to topics each student cares about. Large language
model (LLM) tutors could scale it if they identified students’ interests automatically and
effectively. My dissertation asks, as its abstract puts it: “From what should student interests
be identified?”

## A tutor teaches from what reaches it

A tutor teaches not from the interest itself but from the information about it that reaches the
tutor. So I compared where that information comes from and measured what each source delivers.

## Study 1: asking

Existing transcripts of college students tutored by an interest-based LLM tutor (Dr. Abhishek
Kulkarni’s dataset). Every participant named an interest.

<Stats stats={STUDY1_STATS} />

How much of what students said was knowledge of the interest’s domain, before and after the tutor
used the interest in teaching:

<Figure title="Share of units that were knowledge of the interest’s domain">
  <SplitBar rows={STUDY1_KNOWLEDGE} />
</Figure>

- More disclosure went with higher perceived usefulness of the interest-based session (r = .34;
  0.11 points per unit on a seven-point scale, adjusted), but not significantly with motivation,
  relatedness, or quiz scores. Evidence of a later, individual phase also correlated with
  perceived usefulness.
- Two coders double-coded 25 transcripts and agreed on 90.7% of 700 code-by-transcript
  decisions; code-level Cohen’s κ was .51, and 65 case-level disagreements were resolved under
  written rules.

## Study 2: reading records (IEEE ICALT 2026)

A within-subjects study. An LLM extracted interests, with explanations, from each student’s
samples of browsing history and social-media posts, and students confirmed or rejected each one.

  title="Browsing history vs. social-media posts"

<Stats stats={STUDY2_STATS} />

Posts also placed a smaller share of interests in the early, situational phases, and 54% of the
interests missing from self-report were in the two situational phases. Units count as grounded
under the [grounding check](/research/grounding/). Privacy protocol: participant self-curation,
sensitive-content filtering, and non-retention. The study was supported by NSF CAREER #1942937.

## What it adds up to

<div class="table-wrap">

| Source             | Suits                                       | Evidence                                                                                                             |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Browsing history   | finding the right interest                  | 69.8% of its interests confirmed, vs. 56.3% for posts                                                                |
| Social-media posts | learning about the interest                 | 2.14 vs. 1.58 grounded units per confirmed interest                                                                  |
| Both together      | covering more interests                     | added confirmed interests missing from the self-report form: 38.2% of each student’s validated interests, on average |
| Asking             | the interest in the student’s own words     | every participant named one                                                                                          |
| Teaching           | drawing out what the student knows about it | 88% of the units added later were domain knowledge                                                                   |

</div>

> “Browsing history suits finding the right interest, posts suit learning about it, and both suit
> covering more interests.” “Asking gives the interest in the student’s own words, teaching draws
> out what the student knows about it, and data sources, used with consent, add interests the
> student did not name.”

The appendices publish the materials for reuse: the coding scheme with worked examples and
rules, the survey instrument, and the extraction prompts with synthetic examples.
