# Toward Scalable Interest-Based Learning: Comparing Data Sources for AI-Based Automated Identification of Students' Personal Interests

Nanjie (Jimmy) Rao · Ph.D. dissertation, University of Florida, December 2026 · Chair: Sharon Lynn Chu · Major: Human-Centered Computing

## Abstract

Interest-based learning (IBL) connects instruction to topics each student cares about, which can improve motivation, engagement, and learning. Large language model (LLM) tutors could scale IBL if they identified interests automatically and effectively, without instructor effort for every student. From what should student interests be identified? This dissertation evaluates identification on four constructs: Accuracy, Depth (information beyond an interest’s name), Richness (kinds of information), and interest development Phase. Study 1 introduced the interest information unit (IIU), one fact beyond an interest’s name, to analyze existing transcripts of 35 college students tutored by an interest-based LLM. When asked, students named interests but most said little more. Disclosures conveyed feelings, purposes, knowledge, and engagement; knowledge came mainly during teaching. More disclosure correlated with higher perceived usefulness (𝑟 = .34), but not significantly with motivation, relatedness, or quiz scores. Evidence of a later, individual phase correlated with usefulness. In Study 2, an LLM extracted interests, with explanations, from 32 students’ samples of browsing history and social media posts; students confirmed or rejected each. Browsing history had higher precision (69.8% confirmed, against 56.3% for social media posts). Explanations from posts contained more data-supported information of more kinds per confirmed interest. Students’ follow-up answers placed a smaller share of interests from posts in the early, situational phases.

Both sources added confirmed interests missing from a self-report form. Browsing history suits finding the right interest, posts suit learning about it, and both suit covering more interests. Together, the studies suggest judging identification on Depth, Richness, and Phase as well as Accuracy. They also suggest that asking and the data sources complement each other: asking gives the interest in the student’s own words, teaching draws out what the student knows about it, and data sources, used with consent, add interests the student did not name. The dissertation contributes an account in which a tutor teaches from the information about an interest that reaches the tutor, formalized as the four constructs; the IIU coding scheme, which measures this information in transcripts and LLM explanations; and evidence on what asking, browsing history, and posts each obtain.

## Contributions (Section 1.5)

The dissertation makes four main contributions, stated in full in Chapter 7:
• A theoretical account of identification for IBL, in which a tutor teaches not from the interest itself but from the information about it that reaches the tutor. The account is formalized as a framework of identification effectiveness with four constructs, Accuracy, Depth, Richness, and Phase, each defined apart from its measure (Chapter 3). Building on the four-phase model and on Kulkarni’s observation that interest-based connections were bounded by what elicitation obtained [57, §8.3], the account develops three propositions: what an interest contributes to teaching depends on how much of the student’s relation to it the tutor knows; identification continues during teaching; and the route of identification affects which phases of interest teaching can start from (Section 7.3).

• An instrument for measuring Depth and Richness: the interest-information coding scheme, with its four categories and its unit, the IIU.

• Evidence on what students disclose when an IBL agent asks, and on how their disclosures relate to the outcomes of an interest-based tutoring session. The evidence supports a tentative implication for when IBL can be considered effective (Section 5.10.3).

• A comparison of browsing history and social media posts on the four constructs, within participants and against a self-report baseline, with implications for which data source to use: browsing history, posts, or both (Section 6.7.4).

The coding scheme with its worked examples and rules (Appendix A), the survey instrument (Appendix B), and the extraction prompts with synthetic examples (Appendix C) are offered as reusable research materials.
